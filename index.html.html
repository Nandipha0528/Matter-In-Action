Welcome

Hello, scientist! This course is written for you: a Grade 9 learner who learns Natural Sciences in English, but may think and speak in isiZulu, isiXhosa, Sesotho, Setswana, Sepedi, Afrikaans or another language at home. Your home language is a strength. You may discuss ideas with a partner in any language you share, then write your final answers in English using the word bank and sentence frames on this page.

What you need: this page (phone, or printed by your teacher), a pencil, your exercise book, and simple classroom or kitchen materials such as vinegar, bicarbonate of soda, salt, sugar, water, a candle, and an effervescent tablet. All practical work is done at school with your teacher. No data, internet or help from home is needed.
How each unit works: 1) Read what you will learn. 2) Read the content. 3) Do the activities. 4) Use the resources. 5) Check how you will be assessed. Start with the first level of each task and move up when you feel ready.

Curriculum purpose

This curriculum helps you understand a big CAPS idea: when matter changes, how can we tell whether a new substance has formed, and why does the total mass stay the same? It matters for you because the same ideas explain cooking, rusting, burning, cleaning and medicines.

The standard is the same for every learner. What changes is the route: pictures, practical work, talk, and step-by-step language help so that English is never the thing that stops you from showing what you know. The three units build on each other:

Unit 1 · about 2 weeks

Unit 1: Particles. What is matter made of?

What you will learn (outcomes)

By the end of this unit you can:

  1. Describe the arrangement and movement of particles in solids, liquids and gases using drawings and short sentences.
  2. Explain how the particle model accounts for melting, freezing, evaporating and condensing.
  3. Classify examples as elements, compounds or mixtures, and identify the symbols of the first 20 elements.

Content

  • All matter is made of tiny particles that are always moving.
  • Solids: particles packed closely, vibrate in place. Liquids: close but slide past each other. Gases: far apart, move freely.
  • Heating gives particles more energy, so changes of state happen.
  • Atoms are the smallest units of an element. Elements have one kind of atom (e.g. O, Fe, C). Compounds have different atoms joined (e.g. H2O, NaCl, CO2). Mixtures are not joined (e.g. salt water).

Learning activities

  1. What do I already think? (10 min, in pairs) Talk in any language: Is air matter? Does ice have moving particles? Write one guess each.
  2. Human particle model. The class stands in the aisle or outside. Pack tightly and vibrate (solid), slide around (liquid), spread and move (gas). Your teacher “heats” the class.
  3. Draw and label. Draw the three states and write one sentence under each. Use the sentence frames.
  4. Sorting cards. In mixed-readiness groups of four, sort 15 picture cards into element, compound or mixture and justify each choice.
Start
Label a drawing using the word bank.
Grow
Write full sentences using the frames.
Stretch
Explain why a gas can be squashed but a solid cannot.

Resources

Printed picture cards, word bank (see Help tools), periodic table poster or textbook page, your exercise book. Optional: a short teacher demonstration of ice melting.

How you will be assessed

Diagnostic (start): the “What do I already think?” guesses. Not marked.
Formative (during): exit ticket after each lesson: one thing I understand, one thing I am unsure about.
Summative: Quiz 1 (15 marks). Part A: label three particle drawings (3 marks). Part B: match 6 substances to element, compound or mixture (6 marks). Part C: explain in 2 to 3 sentences why ice melts when heated (6 marks: 2 for particle movement, 2 for energy, 2 for clear sentences). Pictures count as evidence. English errors are not penalised if the science is clear.
Unit 2 · about 2 weeks

Unit 2: Evidence. Physical or chemical change?

What you will learn (outcomes)

  1. Carry out a safe investigation by following instructions and record observations in a table.
  2. Classify changes as physical or chemical using evidence.
  3. Analyse your observations to justify whether a new substance was formed, using claim, evidence and reasoning.

Content

  • Physical change: no new substance (melting, dissolving, tearing). Often easy to reverse.
  • Chemical change: a new substance forms. Evidence can include gas bubbles, colour change, temperature change, a new solid, light or smell. Often hard to reverse.
  • One sign alone is not always proof. Scientists look for more than one piece of evidence.
  • Claim, Evidence, Reasoning (CER): I think… because I saw… which shows…

Learning activities

  1. Station circus (groups of four, teacher supervised, safety glasses or careful distance). Stations: (a) dissolve salt in water, (b) melt a candle stub gently, (c) vinegar plus bicarbonate of soda, (d) effervescent tablet in water. At each station predict, observe, record.
  2. Observation table. Copy the table: Station | What I saw | Sign of new substance? | Reversible? Draw if words are hard.
  3. Talk first, write second. Discuss each station in your group in any language. Then each learner writes their own CER sentence.
  4. Class vote. Each group defends one classification. Other groups ask “What is your evidence?”
Start
Complete a CER paragraph with blanks.
Grow
Write CER with the three starters only.
Stretch
Explain why dissolving sugar is physical even though the sugar seems to “disappear”.

Resources

Station instruction cards with pictures, observation table template, CER starter strip, safety rules poster, everyday materials listed above.

How you will be assessed

Formative: teacher questions at stations (“What do you notice?”), peer feedback using the class vote.
Summative: Practical record and CER task (25 marks). Observation table (8 marks: complete, accurate, labelled). Classification of four changes (8 marks). Individual CER paragraph for station (c) (9 marks: claim 3, evidence 3, reasoning 3). Diagrams, tables and short answers are all valid ways to show understanding.
Unit 3 · about 2 weeks

Unit 3: Reactions. What happens to particles and mass?

What you will learn (outcomes)

  1. Write word equations that show reactants and products for simple reactions.
  2. Explain with particle drawings why the total mass stays the same in a closed reaction.
  3. Evaluate common claims (for example, “burning makes things disappear”) using evidence.
  4. Create a Kitchen Chemistry Explainer that teaches someone at home one reaction.

Content

  • In a chemical reaction, reactants change into products. Example: vinegar + bicarbonate of soda → carbon dioxide + water + sodium acetate.
  • Atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed, so the total mass stays the same (conservation of mass).
  • Gas that escapes into the air explains why an open container seems to lose mass.
  • Reactions around us: rusting, cooking, burning, digestion.

Learning activities

  1. Mass mystery. Teacher weighs a sealed bag with vinegar and bicarbonate before and after mixing (balance if available, or class discussion of the data given on a card). Predict first, then explain.
  2. Particle drawings. Draw reactant and product particles with circles of different colours to show atoms rearranging.
  3. Evaluate the claim. In groups, use a claim card and decide “agree, disagree, partly” with evidence from Units 1 to 3.
  4. Make your explainer. Choose a format and plan it with the checklist. Present to a partner group.
Start
Write one word equation with a template and draw particles.
Grow
Write two word equations and explain mass with drawings.
Stretch
Explain a reaction where gas escapes and predict how to measure it.

Resources

Claim cards, word equation template, particle drawing template, explainer checklist, chart paper or A4 paper, textbook pages, optional balance and sealed bags.

How you will be assessed

Formative: draft feedback from a partner using the checklist, then from the teacher.
Summative: Kitchen Chemistry Explainer (30 marks). Choose a poster, comic strip, short written explanation or a 2-minute spoken explanation (spoken work may begin in your home language, then be summarised in English).

Safety rules
  • Only work with materials your teacher gives you. Never taste or smell directly.
  • Tie back hair. Keep water and candles apart. Wash your hands afterwards.
  • Tell your teacher at once about spills or burns.
Self-check before you hand in
  • Did I answer the question?
  • Did I use at least one piece of evidence?
  • Did I use at least three key words?
